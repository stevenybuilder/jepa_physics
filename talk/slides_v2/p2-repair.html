Source: results/p5_repair_attribution_L12.json, attribution_summary and full.*.none.per_site (16 carrier clips, one held-out target, spline and raw-chord edits, and a random edit of the same norm). The readout is the projection of the residual stream onto the edit direction at every sublayer, as a fraction of the edit's norm, so 1 means the edit is intact and 0 means it is gone. An edit at block 12: attention sublayers of blocks 13 to 16 remove 45 percent of it (sum_attn_contribution minus 0.451, interval minus 0.487 to minus 0.413) and the MLPs 40 percent (minus 0.400), leaving 15 percent after block 16 and 9 percent at block 24. The raw chord is the same, 44 and 39 percent, 18 percent left. A random direction of the same norm is removed faster, 6 percent left after block 16 and 2 percent at 24, with the MLP doing more of it, 51 percent. So the repair is not a decay that treats every direction alike: the model's own direction code is erased more slowly than noise, but it is still erased, by both sublayer types in equal measure, within four blocks. An edit at block 22 keeps 68 percent at block 24: attention in blocks 23 and 24 removes 32 percent and the MLP removes nothing, 0.000, interval within a percent of zero. Two reasons the late edit survives, then: only two blocks remain, and the MLP, which removed 40 percent of the mid-depth edit, has stopped correcting. Patching confirms the attribution: replacing the attention outputs of blocks 13 to 16 with their unedited values leaves more of the edit than replacing the MLP outputs, and both together leave the most. Why this matters: this is the first sublayer attribution of edit wash-out on a video encoder, and it says the mid-depth failure is active correction by both attention and MLP, not passive dilution, which is what the later-layers-repair account from language models predicts. The saddle-axis causal test and a per-block adjacent-layer correction cosine are running and go in the report when they land.
