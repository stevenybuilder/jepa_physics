Part 2 · REPORT §4.4 · 13 / 17

If she asks which I'd use: the subspace to reach a target cheaply, the manifold when the path matters. For a circular variable that means any large shift. Cut from the table, verbatim detail. Subspace strengths: works for scalars and direction alike; 18 probes with the paper's Adam basis; a covariance-weighted rank-2 edit from one probe reaches 3.2°, so the probe count measures the Euclidean edit, and what the learned probes buy is specificity: 0.03 m/s off-target speed change vs 4.2 m/s for a random plane. Subspace limitations: needs a basis chosen on train. Subspace failure: a covariance-weighted edit built from one probe reaches 3°, outside the probe's plane. Spline strengths: closes around the circle; finds its coordinate without labels at point 12 only, through my fallback (Goodfire's own label-free angle fails its periodicity test at point 12); the waypoints are not closer to real clips in 64-D; the 11° forecast steer is 8–13° ahead of the chord at matched edit norm, interpolating variant, and not at the encoder output. Spline limitations: at held-out values the paper's raw-centroid chord lands 5–7° closer than the spline on the headline arc and 1.3° / 2.3° closer averaged over the 16 arcs at points 12 / 22 (the spline ties only the chord between its own smoothed knots); in extrapolation our smoothing spline trails the chord by 0.02–0.06 even continued along the end tangent as the authors' code does (their interpolating arm: better on speed, worse on acceleration). Spline failure: the raw-centroid chord wins the endpoint on the headline arc at both points (4.7° vs 9.7°, 3.6° vs 10.7°); at point 12 no edit reaches the forecast whatever the knot order. Same edit: they agree on the target and disagree on the route. On a line the two methods coincide.
