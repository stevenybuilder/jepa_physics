If she pushes on protocol: her C.12 fits the evaluation probe on the same test clips it steers. I kept that as the headline to stay faithful and put split-half next to it. Off-target: steering direction moves the speed readout by 0.16 to 0.21 (0.49 at N = 10) metres per second, even though the INLP bases are as orthogonal as random ones. The paper's text says under 0.5° with all probes, citing an appendix that reports 11.9° held-out with 20. Cut from the slide: Direction at the paper's layer, held-out evaluation probe, subspace from train, steered clips from test. With a ridge basis one probe fails and five reach the target (three or four under a near-unregularised evaluation probe; the paper gives no recipe), while error-to-true climbs as error-to-target drops. With a refit of her Adam probe sequence as the basis it takes 18, against her ≈ 20 (her basis has 25 probes, ours 84): the count reproduces once the basis does. And a covariance-weighted edit built from one probe steers to 3°, though it leaves the probe's plane and a random plane treated the same way reaches a median 4°, so the along-axis failure is the Euclidean edit and the learned probe adds specificity, not reach. Verdict detail: Against a rank-74 random basis the answer depends on the evaluation probe: the learned basis wins from N = 4 with a near-unregularised probe (α = 1e-3 or Adam; C.12 gives no recipe) and only from N = 9 with my CV-chosen one (α = 100), 200 draws. Against a rank-matched random basis it wins from N = 5 (point 9, 200 draws; N = 3 with those) and N = 2 (point 22, 20 draws); with 200 draws the Adam-built basis beats a same-rank random basis from N = 14 and the rank-2K basis only from N = 30. The stricter split-half evaluation probe gives 12.4° / 17.1° at N = 5 (the two halves) instead of 8.7°, and 4.1° / 4.2° at N = 10. The figure shown is the 20-draw version.
